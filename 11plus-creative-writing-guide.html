📖 Piece 1: Fiction — “The Secret of Willow Creek”

📝 Sample CSSE Prompt: Write a story about a time someone discovered something unexpected.
You have 30 minutes. Write between 250–350 words. Uses: SHAMPOO + ISPACE + DROP
The Secret of Willow Creek

Stumbling through the tangled undergrowth1, Maya pushed aside the curtain of ivy that hid the entrance to Willow Creek.

Beyond the ancient oak trees2, a hidden garden stretched out before her — a forgotten paradise that time itself had overlooked3. The flowers glowed like tiny lanterns in the fading light4, their petals shimmering with colours she had never seen before.

Cautiously5, she stepped forward, her boots crunching6 on the frost-covered path. Crack!6 A twig snapped beneath her foot, and the silence shattered into a thousand pieces7.

“Who’s there?”8 a voice whispered from the shadows. The wind held its breath. The trees leaned closer, their branches reaching down like curious fingers9.

Although her heart was hammering against her ribs10, Maya stood her ground. She was a statue of determination3, rooted to the spot by a courage she did not know she possessed.

Mesmerised11, she watched as a figure emerged from behind the great willow — an old woman with silver hair that cascaded down her shoulders like a waterfall4. Her eyes were older than the mountains themselves7, holding a wisdom that seemed to stretch back to the beginning of time.

“I have been waiting for you,” the woman said, her voice as soft as velvet4. “The garden chose you.”

Could this really be happening? Was she dreaming?12 A deafening silence13 fell over the garden.

Like a key turning in a lock14, something clicked inside Maya’s mind. She felt a sudden, shimmering surge15 of something magical. The leaves rustled and whispered; the brook babbled and burbled6; and the whole garden hummed with an ancient, beautiful energy.

The colours were brighter; the air was sweeter; the world was more alive than she had ever known it to be16. Maya reached out her hand, touched the bark of the great willow, and felt its heartbeat pulse beneath her fingers9. She knew, with absolute certainty, that her life would never be the same again.

Paragraph 1 — Opening
1 ISPACE: I — -ing Opener ISPACE

“Stumbling through…” — Starting with an -ing word immediately puts us in the action. The reader feels like they’re moving with Maya.

Paragraph 2 — Setting
2 ISPACE: P — Preposition ISPACE

“Beyond the ancient oak trees…” — A preposition opener tells us where things are, painting the scene before the main clause.

3 SHAMPOO: M — Metaphor SHAMPOO

“a forgotten paradise” — The garden is not literally a paradise. A metaphor says something IS something else (no “like” or “as”).

4 SHAMPOO: S — Simile SHAMPOO

“glowed like tiny lanterns” — Uses “like” to compare flowers to lanterns. Also used later: “like a waterfall”, “as soft as velvet.”

Paragraph 3 — Tension Builds
5 ISPACE: A — Adverb ISPACE

“Cautiously,” — An adverb opener tells us how something is done. Notice the comma after it!

6 SHAMPOO: O — Onomatopoeia SHAMPOO DROP: O

“Crunching”, “Crack!”, “rustled”, “babbled”, “hummed” — Words that sound like what they describe. Makes the reader hear the scene!

7 SHAMPOO: H — Hyperbole SHAMPOO

“shattered into a thousand pieces” — Obvious exaggeration for dramatic effect. Also: “older than the mountains.”

Paragraph 4 — Mystery
8 DROP: D — Dialog DROP

“Who’s there?” — Dialog hooks the reader instantly. Notice the correct punctuation: speech marks, question mark inside, then the speaking verb.

9 SHAMPOO: P — Personification SHAMPOO

“The wind held its breath” — Wind can’t breathe! Giving human actions to nature makes the setting feel alive and eerie. Also: “felt its heartbeat.”

Paragraph 5 — Courage
10 ISPACE: C — Conjunction ISPACE

“Although her heart was hammering…” — A conjunction opener builds a contrast: she was scared BUT she stood her ground. Creates tension.

Paragraph 6 — The Encounter
11 ISPACE: E — -ed Opener ISPACE

“Mesmerised, she watched…” — An -ed word tells us Maya’s emotional state before describing what she sees. Very effective!

Paragraph 7 — Shock
12 DROP: R — Rhetorical Questions DROP

“Could this really be happening?” — We don’t expect an answer. Rhetorical questions show the character’s inner thoughts and pull the reader in.

13 SHAMPOO: O — Oxymoron SHAMPOO

“deafening silence” — Two opposite words together! “Deafening” means extremely loud, but “silence” means no sound. The clash creates a powerful image.

Paragraph 8 — Magic
14 ISPACE: S — Simile Opener ISPACE

“Like a key turning in a lock…” — Opening with a simile paints a picture before the main idea. The reader “sees” the comparison first.

15 SHAMPOO: A — Alliteration SHAMPOO

“sudden, shimmering surge” — Three words starting with the same ‘s’ sound. It sounds smooth and magical — the rhythm matches the meaning!

Paragraph 9 — Powerful Ending
16 Rule of Three + Semicolons SHAMPOO ;

“brighter; sweeter; more alive” — Three parallel clauses separated by semicolons. The rhythm of three feels complete and powerful. Examiners love this!

★ DROP: P — Action Ending DROP

The story ends with Maya doing something — reaching out, touching the bark — not just thinking. Action endings are more powerful than “and then I woke up”!

Examiner Top Tips for Fiction: ✅ Use all 5 senses | ✅ Show, don’t tell | ✅ Vary openers (ISPACE) | ✅ Include dialog | ✅ End with a memorable final line

📣 Piece 2: Non-Fiction — “Why Every School Should Have a Garden”

📝 Sample CSSE Prompt: Write a letter to your headteacher persuading them to make a change at your school.
You have 30 minutes. Write between 250–350 words. Uses: A FOREST + Colon & Semicolon
Why Every School Should Have a Garden

Dear Mrs Thompson,

Have you ever wondered what it would be like if every child could grow their own food, breathe fresh air during school hours, and learn lessons that no textbook could ever teach?1

I passionately believe that every school in Britain deserves a school garden2. It is, without doubt, one of the most important changes we could make to transform the lives of young people3.

A green, growing garden4 would give students a space to learn, relax, and reconnect with nature. According to the Royal Horticultural Society, school gardens improve children’s understanding of science by allowing them to observe plant life cycles, soil ecosystems, and weather patterns first-hand5.

Astonishingly, 73% of children in a recent NHS survey said they felt calmer and happier after spending time outdoors during the school day6.

A school garden teaches patience; it builds responsibility; it nurtures a love of learning that lasts a lifetime7.

Imagine a child who struggles in the classroom — a child who feels lost among equations and essays — suddenly discovering that they have a gift for growing things. That moment of pride, that spark of confidence3: it could change everything8.

Can we really afford to deny our children this opportunity?1

The benefits are clear:8 better mental health, stronger science skills, and a deeper connection to the world around us7.

Surely, it is our solemn, sacred duty4 to provide the best possible education for every single child. I firmly believe that a school garden is not a luxury — it is a necessity2. Let us plant the seeds of a brighter future, starting today.

Yours sincerely,
[Your Name]

Opening — Hook the Reader
1 A FOREST: R — Rhetorical Question A FOREST

“Have you ever wondered…?” — A question you don’t expect an answer to. It makes the reader stop and think. Great way to start a persuasive piece! Used again later: “Can we really afford…?”

Paragraph 2 — State Your View
2 A FOREST: O — Opinion A FOREST

“I passionately believe…” — Your personal view. Use phrases like “I believe”, “I am convinced”, “In my view.” State it confidently! Also used at the end: “I firmly believe.”

3 A FOREST: E — Emotive Language A FOREST

“transform the lives of young people” — Words chosen to stir feelings. Also: “struggles”, “lost”, “pride”, “spark of confidence.” These make the reader care about your argument.

Paragraph 3 — Evidence
4 A FOREST: A — Alliteration A FOREST

“green, growing garden” — Repeating the same starting sound. It’s catchy and memorable. Also: “solemn, sacred.”

5 A FOREST: F — Fact A FOREST

“According to the Royal Horticultural Society…” — A true, verifiable statement from a real organisation. Facts make your argument trustworthy. Name the source!

Paragraph 4 — Numbers
6 A FOREST: S — Statistic A FOREST

“73% of children…” — A number or percentage that backs up your point. Statistics sound researched and scientific. Even in the exam, you can make up a plausible-sounding one!

Paragraph 5 — Power of Three
7 A FOREST: T — Rule of Three A FOREST

“patience; responsibility; a love of learning” — Three things listed together for rhythm and completeness. Three feels “just right.” Used twice — once with semicolons, once with commas.

Paragraph 6 — Emotional Appeal
8 Colon & Semicolon Punctuation

Semicolon (;) — joins two closely related sentences: “teaches patience; builds responsibility.”

Colon (:) — introduces a dramatic reveal or list: “spark of confidence: it could change everything.” Also: “The benefits are clear:”

Using these correctly shows the examiner you are writing at a high level!

Closing — Call to Action
★ Strong Ending TIP

“Let us plant the seeds of a brighter future” — A call to action tells the reader what to do. The clever metaphor links “planting seeds” (the garden) to “a brighter future” (hope). Always end with power!

Examiner Top Tips for Non-Fiction: ✅ Correct format (Dear… / Yours sincerely) | ✅ Hit ALL of A FOREST | ✅ Use colons and semicolons | ✅ End with a call to action

🎯 Exam Day Reminders

⏰ Time: 5 mins planning, 20 mins writing, 5 mins checking.

📝 Plan: Jot down which techniques you will use (tick them off!).

✅ Paragraphs: At least 4–5. Each one has a clear purpose.

🏆 Opening: Hook the reader in the first sentence (use DROP or ISPACE!).

🏁 Closing: End with a powerful, memorable final sentence.

🔍 SPaG: Read through once at the end for Spelling, Punctuation, Grammar.

✨ Vocabulary: Use ambitious words — “cautiously” not “carefully”, “cascaded” not “fell”.
